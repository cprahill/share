Slot Three

story-lord · Marty Brown · private reading draft

Len kept the van in third because fourth made the CRT blink. The dash was analog: oil, amps, a cassette deck with a red tooth that ate the edge of every tape she fed it. The CRT sat where a radio would have been. It drew a two-lane and a blinking square twenty-eight miles out. RIDGE HUB, it said, then the square, then a number that ticked down like a parking meter.

The packet was in the passenger footwell in a plastic case that had once held a mix. Label in grease pencil: `SLOT 3 / DO NOT PLAY`. She did not play it. She drove.

The mall still had a name on the big sign, letters missing so it read like a cough. Inside, the air was mall air: cold, sweet, a little burnt. The carpet was that gray-and-pink maze they never replaced because replacing it would mean admitting the place was still open. A floor machine hummed two corridors over. Someone was still getting paid to keep the pattern.

The arcade was the last lit box. Six cabinets. Five of them dark. One ran: RIDGE RUN, yellow letters, a steering yoke instead of a stick. The screen was a CRT with a burn where the horizon had sat for years. High scores down the right, ten names, ten numbers. Not initials. Node IDs. `HQT-2`. `LOT-A`. `VAN-9`. Slot three was a gap. The number next to the gap was hers, waiting.

A kid in a work shirt sat on a folding chair with a coil of keys. He did not look at her face. He looked at the cassette case.

“You play or you drop,” he said.

“Drop is play,” Len said.

He shrugged. The keys chimed. “Machine knows. I just lock up.”

She fed the cassette into the slot under the yoke. The red tooth took it. RIDGE RUN did not show a road. It showed a service lane behind the mall, dumpsters, a chain-link cut, the same two-lane she had just driven, only from above, like a security cam that had learned to be a game. Her job was to keep the van-shape in the lane until the meter hit zero and the gap in the list filled.

She could do that. She had done worse with less picture.

The yoke pulled left on the same pothole every lap. She let it. Fighting it made the CRT smear. She counted the dumpsters. She did not look at the kid. At zero the machine thunked, the kind of thunk a locker makes. Slot three filled: `LEN-4`.

The kid stood up. “Packet’s in,” he said. “You’re done.”

Len waited for the cassette to spit. It did not. The screen went to a service menu. White on black. `ROUTE CERTIFICATE`. Her van’s plate. Her meter. A timestamp from two towns back, 04:12, while she had been asleep in a rest lot with the oil light on.

`REPLACED BY LEN-5`

`RECALL: THIS NODE`

The kid was already at the glass door with the keys.

“You knew,” she said.

“I know slot three,” he said. “I don’t know you. There’s a Len-5 in the lot. Came in on four. Same van paint. Different tooth on the deck.”

She walked to the lot. There was a van. Same dings. Same third-gear rattle when the other driver bumped it into idle. A woman with Len’s jacket cut sat in it eating a gas-station hot dog, CRT glowing the next square. She did not wave. She did not have to. The packet had been a recall. Len had delivered it herself because that was the job and the job did not care which Len still thought she was the original.

Len went back in. The cabinet had already eaten the cassette. RIDGE RUN was a road again, waiting for the next drop. Slot three still said `LEN-4`. The gap was gone. She was on the board. That was the whole prize.

She asked the kid if she could buy a blank tape. He sold her one from a drawer of blanks that had never been blanks, just wiped. She paid cash. She did not ask for a receipt. Out at her van the analog dash was already ticking. The deck’s red tooth was turning with nothing in it, chewing air, logging a dead route because that was what the tooth did when the certificate was gone.

Len sat with the door open. The mall sign coughed. The other van pulled out toward the next square. She could have followed. She could have rammed it. She could have gone back in and kicked the yoke until slot three blanked.

She put the wiped cassette in the deck. It took. The CRT drew the two-lane with no square. Just road. She started in third. The oil light stayed off for a mile, then came back, same as always.

She did not smash the deck. She did not call anyone. She drove the unlit stretch until the CRT found a different blink, smaller, unlabeled, and she did not know if it was a hub or a hole. She kept third. The tooth kept eating the tape. Behind her, slot three held.

The next town would treat her as empty. She was still moving. That was not the same thing as a win, and she did not say it out loud.

The Float

story-lord · Marty Brown · private reading draft

Hask kept the booth lamp on the low click because the high click made the CRT smear. The tube sat where a ticket window used to be, four lag screws and a washer worn to a thumbprint. It did not draw a road. It drew a ledger: bay, minutes, plate, a hash that burned into the phosphor if you left a line up too long.

The rest lot was nine bays and a gravel apron on a two-lane that did not keep a name on the map. One sodium still live over the apron. The other had been a cone of glass in the gravel since last winter. The booth was a box: sliding pane, hot plate, a stool with a split vinyl seat, a drawer of log slips, the stamp.

A log slip was a cassette. Drivers slid it through the pane. Hask fed it to the deck under the tube. The tooth took the edge. The CRT wrote rest onto the tape — bay, minutes, hash — and the cassette came back warm. That was the ticket. Without it the two-lane counted idle as a violation. The lot's arm would not lift on the way out unless the tape had a live hash.

Hask had the job because he could keep the stamp honest. First hour of the shift the tooth chewed a log and would not spit. He popped the deck with a plastic spudger, pulled the tape, trimmed the chewed edge with the booth scissors, reseated the pinch roller. The lag screw on the lower left walked if you ignored it; he snugged it with the same wrench he kept under the hot plate. Service menu, white on black: `STAMP TEST`, `FLOAT`, `CLERK ID`. He had been on `CLERK ID` for eleven months. The float was lot credit. Hours the company bought so the bays could fill without the tube going red.

A reefer took bay two just after the sodium flickered. The driver had a split knuckle and a log that had already been through two other stamps, the tape edge fuzzy. Hask fed it. Forty minutes, bay two. The float ticked from ninety to fifty. He passed the cassette back. She put it in the same pocket as her gloves and did not wait for the pane to close.

The float had started the shift at four hundred. The afternoon clerk had left without wiping the last hash; a ghost bar still sat in the burn if you looked from the side. That was usual. The drawer held a company cassette, grease pencil: `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. He had used it twice this week. It was what you did when the ledger got thin and the lot still had trucks.

A van held third across the apron and did not take a bay. Dash CRT, empty slot chewing air. It did not stop. The arm was up for through-traffic. Hask wrote nothing.

A box truck took bay six with a log that wanted three hours. The tube put up a red bar: `FLOAT LOW`. Hask fed the top-up cassette. The tooth took it like any other slip. The float jumped to four hundred. He stamped the box truck: three hours, bay six, hash. The driver left with the tape in his shirt pocket like a cigarette he was saving.

The CRT did not go back to the ledger. It sat on a line Hask had not keyed.

`CLERK HOURS APPLIED` `REMAINING: 0` `HASK — REST COMPLETE` `BAY: BOOTH`

He opened the service menu. He could always get there. `STAMP TEST` fired a blank hash onto a wiped slip. The tube still stamped. The float still read four hundred. `CLERK ID` now carried a rest ticket, same format as a truck. The top-up had not been company hours. It had been his remaining shift, spent into the lot so bay six could sleep. He had not agreed to that. He had agreed to a wage and a stool.

He tried `VOID`. `VOID` wanted a clerk with remaining hours. He was the clerk. He was spent.

The pane still opened. A tanker tapped the glass with a ring. Hask took the cassette, fed it, and the tube refused: `CLERK FULL`. He tried the lag screw. He tried the spudger. He reseated the tooth. The tooth was fine. The stamp was fine. It would not spend a spent clerk.

He put the tanker on the hot-plate side of the counter with a paper square, old method, minutes in pencil. The driver looked at it like a joke that had arrived late. The arm would not take pencil. The tanker idled on the apron until the minutes ran out of the driver instead of the tape, then left the way it had come, arm still down, two-lane treating it as what it was.

Hask could have pulled the tube's power and let the lot go dark. Dawn would have found a dead stamp and a clerk who had made a point. He did not. He ran `STAMP TEST` until the hash printed clean. He left the float at four hundred. He left himself on the rest line.

A local slid a cassette through and waited. Hask shook his head. The local took bay nine with the engine off, which was not legal and was quieter than the pane.

Light came up the two-lane the color of the hot-plate coil. The dawn clerk's hatchback turned in and parked in the gravel that was not a bay. Hask had the pane locked and the menu back on the ledger. The tube was ready. The clerk ticket was still his. He sat on the split stool with a rest authorization for a bay that was the booth, already complete, and he did not get off it until the hatchback door shut and the other set of keys hit the counter.
