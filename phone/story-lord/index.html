Slot Three

story-lord · Marty Brown · Heavy rewrite

The packet on the passenger floor said `SLOT 3 / DO NOT PLAY`.

Len did not play it. She was taking it to RIDGE HUB. The drop paid if the board took the cassette and filled a gap. That was the job. Lose the gap and you were a van with a tape and no route.

The CRT where a radio used to live drew a two-lane and a blinking square. RIDGE HUB. Under the square a meter ticked the way a parking meter ticked when a city still wanted coins. The square was the job board. The meter was how long the board would still admit she was coming.

She kept the van in third because fourth smeared the picture. Analog dash: oil, amps, a deck with a red tooth that logged every edge it ate. The tooth was the logger. A hub that liked the log kept you on the square. A hub that did not like the log still kept the log.

She had already done a drop today. It had spat. It had paid. The square on the tube had held.

Slot 6 was a wash tunnel that still ran after the grocery beside it went dark. The cabinet was bolted to the token box. The packet had said `SLOT 6 / NO SOAP`. She fed it. The tunnel CRT came up from the ceiling: brushes, rails, a van-shape already nosed into the mouth. Her job was to keep that shape between the rails until the meter died and to stay out of the soap cycle, which whites the tube and freezes the count.

The first brush hit on a delay. She steered for where the rail would be, not where the phosphor put it. The van-shape hopped and stayed in. Soap heads along the wall stayed dry. The token box ticked like a clock that only knew one job.

A real truck rolled the real apron outside. The cam did not show it. The yoke still pulled when the real truck's wake hit the tunnel mouth. She let the pull. Fighting it smeared the rails the way fourth smeared her own tube.

At twelve the soap heads twitched. The packet had said no soap. She lifted off and held the shape short of the trip plate. The heads settled. Meter: eight. She put the shape back on the rail and did not look at the attendant through the glass. Attendants watched tokens. They did not watch faces.

Zero. The cabinet thunked. Slot 6 filled with a node ID that was not hers. The cassette spat back, edge chewed, hubs warm. The attendant counted thirty onto the stainless without looking up and punched a hash on a slip. The CRT in her dash kept the hub square. She was still the Len who owned the route.

She asked if a clean leader paid extra. It did not. Thirty was thirty. She put the chewed tape in the door pocket with the other chewed tapes and took the cash. The tunnel was already a dark tube. The next courier could have it.

She slept two hours on a gravel shoulder with the oil light on. She killed the CRT to save amps. She left the tooth empty so it would not chew a tape she might still need. She woke with this packet already in the footwell and RIDGE HUB already blinking, meter live. She did not remember writing `SLOT 3 / DO NOT PLAY`. Couriers woke up with packets. Slot 6 had paid and spat. Slot 3 would pay the same way if she fed the tape and filled the gap.

Twenty-eight miles. Then twenty-four. Mid-drive the square flickered, dropped a frame, came back one tick leaner than it should have been. She did not punch the tube. Punching a CRT taught it nothing. She stayed in third and watched the two-lane in the windshield agree with the two-lane on the glass. The flicker did not come back. The meter kept dying the honest way.

She stopped for water at an all-hours window. The night clerk saw the case and put a palm on it like a man who collected unclaimed drops. She kept her hand on the spine.

“That’s a play tape,” he said. “Slot three. We buy those.”

“It’s a drop.”

“Drops play. That’s why they print DO NOT.” He named a price that was more than Slot 6 had paid. She did not sell it. Selling it would leave her on the two-lane with a live meter and nothing to feed the board. He sold her the water. He looked at the case until the van door shut.

The mall still had a name on the big sign. Letters missing. Inside, a floor machine hummed. Someone was still getting paid to keep the pattern.

The arcade was the last lit box. RIDGE RUN in yellow letters. A steering yoke instead of a stick. The screen was a CRT. High scores down the right: names, numbers, node IDs. Slot three was a gap. The number next to the gap was waiting.

A kid in a work shirt sat on a folding chair with a coil of keys. He looked at the cassette case, not at her face.

“You play or you drop,” he said.

“Drop is play,” Len said.

He shrugged. The keys chimed. “Machine knows. I just lock up.”

She set the case on the cabinet lip and popped it. The tape inside had her handwriting on the leader, or handwriting that looked like hers. The red tooth on her dash was a logger. This slot was a mouth. She fed the cassette under the yoke. The tooth in the cabinet took the edge the way hers did, a little too eager, a little too much plastic in the bite.

RIDGE RUN did not show a road. It showed the service dock behind the mall: a roll-up door, standing water, the same two-lane she had just driven, only from above, like a security cam that had learned to be a game. A van-shape sat at the mouth of the dock, her dings copied onto its quarter panel. Her job was to keep that shape in the lane until the meter hit zero and the gap in the list filled.

The yoke had weight. Not arcade weight. Drop weight.

Lap one the roll-up started down. A timer in the corner of the cam ran a two-count she had not been told about. Under the door when it hit the count, the tube went white and the meter froze. She had frozen a meter two towns back and sat there until the hub timed out and paid nothing. She lifted and held short of the threshold. The door kissed the concrete and rattled up again. The van-shape stayed out. Meter: still fat.

Standing water on the dock floor read as lane paint. The cam wanted her in it. The real floor, if she had walked it, was a slick. She put the shape on the dry edge and let the phosphor argue. The meter did not freeze. The cam did not thank her.

The kid’s keys were quiet. They only chimed when the board wrote a node. Nothing had written yet.

Lap two a night porter pushed the floor machine across the far end of the dock. Real sound came through the cinderblock a half-second before the cam put him in the lane. She steered for the sound. The van-shape missed him. The machine left a wet stripe the cam tried to call a wall. She did not treat it as a wall.

The yoke had a dead-zone at two o’clock. Past that the picture smeared whether you were in third in your head or not. She kept the pull short of the zone. The van-shape had her rust at the wheel well. It had the scrape on the bumper from a dock in a town that did not put its name on the CRT. She was driving a picture of her own van around a picture of a dock she had already parked behind. That was the job. The packet went in. The gap filled. You got to keep the route.

Lap three the pothole arrived before the picture. She had felt it in the van on the way in. The cam showed it late. She steered for where it would be. The van-shape hopped and stayed in. A parked courier at the far cone sat in the cam as traffic. The cam wanted her to wait or clip it. Clipping it smeared the CRT the way fourth did. She waited. The parked van did not move. It was not going to move. She took the long way around the cone and paid the seconds.

Meter: half.

The kid said nothing. He was waiting on the machine, not on her.

Lap four she almost overcorrected. The roll-up timer started while she was still in the water the cam called paint. She yanked off the two-o’clock dead-zone and the phosphor bloomed. She dropped her hands. The yoke found center. The van-shape had kissed the cone. The meter had not frozen. She put the shape back on the dry edge and did not look at the kid.

“Still in,” he said, to the keys.

The meter got thin. Twelve. Eight. She kept third in her head even though there was no gearbox on the cabinet. Fourth smeared CRTs. This was a CRT. She did not ask it for more picture than it had.

Five. Three. The roll-up stayed up. The porter was gone. The standing water kept lying. At zero the machine thunked, the kind of thunk a locker makes when it takes a bag and does not give it back.

Slot three filled: `LEN-4`.

The kid’s keys chimed. He stood up. “Packet’s in,” he said. “You’re done.”

Len waited for the cassette to spit.

It did not.

Slot 6 had spat. Tunnels spat. You got the chewed tape back as proof you had been the drop, and you put it in the door pocket with the others. She waited a breath. Another. The tooth in the cabinet kept its bite. The hubs did not reverse.

She hit the eject under the yoke. The button had travel. The tooth kept turning. The tape stayed in the mouth, leader gone, hubs still. She hit it again. Same travel. Same nothing. She put a thumbnail in the slot the way you did when a deck ate a mix you still wanted. There was no purchase. The plastic was flush.

She went out to the van and came back with the flat screwdriver she used on the dash bezel. The kid watched the glass door, not the tool. She worked the slot. The cabinet tooth had the tape, and the tape was the packet, and the packet was in. The screwdriver found no lip. She put it in her pocket.

“It’ll spit,” she said.

“Packet’s in,” the kid said. “That’s how slot three works. I just lock up.”

“I need the tape.”

“Machine kept it.” He was already looking at the glass. “You’re done.”

The screen did not go back to the dock. It did not go back to RIDGE RUN. It went to a service menu, white on black, the same ugly font her dash used when she held the mode for three seconds.

`ROUTE CERTIFICATE`

Her van’s plate, letter for letter, including the dented character the county had never restamped. Her meter, the number that had just died. A timestamp from the shoulder where she had slept: 04:12. Oil light on. Slot 6 cash in the jacket. This packet not yet in the footwell, or already in the footwell and she had not looked. Year on the line like a stamp the tube did not think needed saying: 2442.

`REPLACED BY LEN-5`

`RECALL: THIS NODE`

The kid was at the glass door with the keys.

“You knew,” she said.

“I know slot three,” he said. “I don’t know you. There’s a Len-5 in the lot. Came in on four. Same van paint. Different tooth on the deck.”

He locked the door from the inside and then unlocked it, a habit, and went out. The coil of keys stayed loud. He did not wait to see if she followed. Slot three was full. His job was to lock up when it filled.

Len looked at the menu until the phosphor made a bar of the words. `ROUTE CERTIFICATE` did not ask her to confirm. It had already confirmed. The timestamp was a sleep she remembered. Someone had written a packet in that dark, or the deck had, or the hub had, and she had driven it here because that was the drop.

She walked to the lot.

There was a van. Same dings. Same rust at the wheel well. Same scrape from the dock that did not put its name on a CRT. Same third-gear rattle when the other driver bumped it into idle. A woman with Len’s jacket cut sat in it with both hands on the wheel. The CRT in that dash already held the next square, live, blinking, a meter fat enough to travel. On the dash sat a cassette in grease pencil. The hand on the spine was closer to the Slot 3 hand than Len’s current hand was. The deck in that dash had a tooth. It was red. It was not Len’s red. Newer plastic, less chew in the notch, a clean bite that had not yet learned every tape.

The other woman’s cuff had a stitch Len’s cuff did not. Same jacket pattern. Different repair.

Len walked as far as the stall paint and stopped. The other van’s oil light was off. Hers, in the next stall, was already ticking the way it ticked when the engine was cold. Same paint fade on the sliding door. Same crease above the wheel. The bumper scrape lined up if you squinted and did not if you did not: a little longer on the other van, or a little newer, metal still bright in the groove.

She almost spoke. The name was in her mouth. The plate was in her mouth. She did not say either. The other woman did not wave. She did not have to. She had a square on her CRT. Len’s CRT, through the glass of her own van, still showed RIDGE HUB with the meter at zero.

The packet had been a recall. Len had delivered it herself because that was the job, and the job did not care which Len still thought she was the original.

The other van’s idle was her idle. The dings lined up. The tooth did not. That was the proof. She could have asked for a face and gotten one. She did not ask. The other woman looked at the next square, not at the stall, and reached for the gear without looking at the lot. Third. Always third. The rattle was the rattle. The tooth in that deck sat still, a packet already on the dash and not yet in the mouth. Len’s tooth, empty, had started to turn.

Len went back in. The cabinet had already eaten the cassette. RIDGE RUN was a dock again, a clean lane, waiting for the next drop. Slot three still said `LEN-4`. The gap was gone. She was on the board.

The kid had not gone far. He was locking a grate that did not need locking. She asked if she could buy the name off the board.

He looked at slot three, then at her, still not at her face. “Board doesn’t sell names,” he said. “I sell tape.”

She asked for a blank. He sold her one from a drawer of blanks that had never been blanks, just wiped. The leader was too white. The hubs had no dust. She paid cash from the Slot 6 thirty. She did not ask for a receipt. He did not offer one. He looked at the empty slot under the yoke, which was empty now, which was his problem until the next courier.

Out at her van the analog dash was already ticking. Oil. Amps. The deck’s red tooth was turning with nothing in it, chewing air, logging a dead route because that was what the tooth did when the certificate was gone. She sat with the door open and listened to it. It had a rhythm. It had always had a rhythm. Now the rhythm had no tape to spend.

She put the wiped cassette in the deck. It took. The tooth found the white leader and started eating what had already been eaten once. For one pass the CRT wrote `LEN-4` onto the leader in the same ugly font the cabinet had used. Then the letters dropped out, phosphor by phosphor, because a spent certificate does not hold a name on a wiped tape. No hash. No plate. No meter. The logger had a tape and nothing legal to put on it.

The CRT drew the two-lane with no square. Just road. No RIDGE HUB. No meter. The job board had let her go by taking her. She sat long enough to see if the square would come back if she waited. It did not. The two-lane on the tube was only the two-lane. The windshield agreed.

The other van pulled out toward the next square. Same third. Same rattle. She started in third. Fourth would smear a picture she no longer needed, and she kept the habit anyway.

The oil light stayed off for a mile, then came back, same as always. She did not smash the deck. She did not call anyone. There was no number on a recall that wanted the person it had just spent.

She drove the unlit stretch with the tooth working. The wiped tape had no route on it. It still had an edge. The tooth still wanted the edge. When the first pass ran out the hubs clicked and the tooth chewed air and then found the leader again, because a wiped tape is all leader if you never recorded a route.

The CRT found a different blink, smaller, unlabeled. It sat off the two-lane like a hub and like a hole. She did not know which. A live certificate would have given her a meter. The meter stayed gone. The blink stayed a blink. She did not put it in a count she no longer had.

Behind her, slot three held `LEN-4`. The board still had her name. The certificate did not.

The next town would treat her as empty. She was still moving. She kept third. The unlabeled blink got a little closer. On the wiped pass the tooth clicked the way it clicked when a hub was about to write a hash, then wrote nothing, then clicked again. The oil light held. She drove.

The Float

story-lord · Marty Brown · Heavy rewrite

Hask stamped driver cassettes so the arm would lift. That was the job. The CRT in the booth was the ledger: bay, minutes, plate, a hash. Without a live hash the two-lane counted idle as a violation, and the lot’s arm stayed down on the way out.

The float kept the tube from going red. Float was hours. Hours somebody owned. The company bought them so the bays could fill. A clerk with remaining hours could stamp. A clerk without them could not. The tube did not put that on the hire sheet. It put it on the phosphor when the number ran out.

He kept the booth lamp on the low click because the high click smeared the tube. Nine bays on a gravel apron. One sodium live over the plates.

A log slip was a cassette. Drivers slid it through the pane. Hask fed it to the deck under the tube. The tooth took the edge. The CRT wrote rest onto the tape — bay, minutes, hash — and the cassette came back warm. Warm was live. Cold was a lie you could still hand through glass if you wanted the pane empty more than you wanted the stamp true. He did not want the pane empty that badly.

He had the job because he could keep the stamp honest.

First hour a sleeper in bay five pushed a tape with a leader that had fuzzed from a dirty deck two lots back. The county overlay in the corner of the tube offered `AUTO-CLEAR PLATE` in a polite bar, the kind of polite that wanted him to tap and go back to the stool. Auto-clear would print a hash off the plate camera and skip the tooth. The arm would lift. The two-lane would argue the slip at the next scale because the hash would not match the leader.

Hask did not tap the bar. He took the spudger from the mug on the sill and trimmed the fuzz off the leader, one pass, enough for the tooth to find plastic instead of lint. He fed the tape. The overlay bar died, offended. The CRT wrote bay five, forty minutes, hash. The cassette came back warm. He passed it. The driver put it in the dash deck without looking, which was correct. The arm believed the hash, not the face. The arm lifted. Float ticked 400 to 360. The stamp was honest. The hours were somebody’s. He did not ask whose.

Service menu, white on black: `STAMP TEST`, `FLOAT`, `CLERK ID`. He had been on `CLERK ID` for eleven months. `FLOAT` read three hundred sixty after the sleeper. Still a night. `CLERK ID` had hours left on it. He meant to take those home as a wage.

He did not open the drawer. The drawer held the company cassette. He did not need it while the number was fat.

Ordinary work after that. Pickup in bay one, fifteen minutes. A refresh on a reefer already legal, ten minutes, just enough so the two-lane would not argue an old slip. Float came down in small bites. The pane stayed easy. The arm stayed a machine that believed him.

The afternoon clerk had left a ghost bar in the burn if you looked from the side. Uncleared hash. One line. Hask did not wipe it. Wiping it meant a PIN the day stool had not left. The ghost would spend itself. Uncleared lines did that.

The speaker on the sill popped once. A box, twenty minutes out, produce, three hours, a dock that would not take brown leaf. Hask wrote `3HR` on the counter in grease pencil so he would not get to pretend he had not heard.

A tanker took bay two after the sodium flickered. Forty minutes. Tape already through two other stamps. He fed it. The tooth chewed, then took. Forty, bay two, hash. Cassette warm enough. The float dropped from ninety to fifty when the ghost bar finished burning on the same tick as the hash.

He looked at the number twice. The clean stamps had not spent that much. The ghost had. Fifty left. Fifty was not a night.

Two drivers hit the pane at the same time.

Day cab, bay eight, a log that wanted an hour. Behind him a flatbed with a steel load and a dock stamp due at the mill before first break. The flatbed wanted forty. The tube had fifty. Somebody was going to sit dark or sit a lie.

The day-cab driver got his cassette into the slot first and held it so the pane could not close. The flatbed driver put a folded twenty on the sill under the glass, not looking at it, the way you did when you wanted a hash more than you wanted a receipt.

“I was here,” the day cab said.

“I’ve got a mill clock,” the flatbed said. “Forty. You can see the load.”

Hask did not take the twenty. Cash under the glass bought a hash that would not match a ledger line. The arm would lift. The mill scale would not. He pushed the bill back with the side of the spudger.

“Fifty on the float,” Hask said. “I can stamp one of you honest. I cannot stamp both. I will not stamp a forty with ten of lie on the end.”

“Stamp me fifty and I’ll sit the rest,” the day cab said.

“You’ll sit with a hash that dies at fifty. Two-lane still counts the rest. Arm won’t lift you legal.”

“Then stamp him forty and give me the ten,” the day cab said. “I’ll take a sliver.”

“A ten-minute hash on an hour sit is the same lie in a smaller wrap.”

The flatbed left the twenty on the sill. “Mill fines me if I’m late. Lot fines me if I’m dark. You’re the clerk.”

“I’m the stamp,” Hask said. “The stamp is fifty. One ticket.”

He did not feed either cassette. He set both on the counter, day-cab tape on the left, flatbed on the right, and let the pane sit open. Fifty was not red yet. Fifty was not a night. A dishonest hash was a warm cassette with a short future. He did not hand those.

The day cab idled in bay eight without a hash. Illegal. Quieter than a fight. The flatbed stayed on the apron with the steel ticking as it cooled. The twenty stayed on the sill until the flatbed driver took it back, embarrassed at the plastic.

Hask could have stamped the forty and let the two-lane sort the day cab. That was a clerk who wanted the pane empty. He wanted the stamp honest.

The drawer held a company cassette, grease pencil: `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. He had used it once this week, Tuesday, when a wreck west of the river stacked three sleepers and the float kissed red at twenty-two hundred. He fed the tape. The float jumped. The arm kept lifting. He got off the stool at dawn with a wage. He had understood the hours as company hours. A card on the sill said `TOP UP AUTHORIZED FLOAT < 100`. Authorized had felt like company.

He looked at the cassette and did not take it yet. Fifty was not zero. Zero was when you had no choice.

A van held third across the apron and did not take a bay. Dash CRT, empty slot chewing air. The arm was up. Hask wrote nothing.

The box truck took bay six while the other two were still deciding who was owed the fifty. Produce unit already cycling. The driver slid a log that wanted three hours like a man who had already paid. The tube put up a red bar: `FLOAT LOW`.

Fifty would not cover three hours. Fifty would not cover an hour.

“Three hours,” the driver said. “Leaf. Dock won’t take it brown.”

Hask showed him the bar. The driver had seen red bars. He had not come here to sit in one.

“I stamp you three off fifty, the hash dies before your window, and the arm still won’t lift you legal. You sit a lie.”

The driver put his palm on the pane. “Then don’t sit me a lie. Top it up. This booth tops up. I ran here last Tuesday.”

Last Tuesday was the wreck tape. The driver had a memory for which lots still sold hours.

The day cab was out of the cab now. The flatbed driver stood in the sodium with him. Both of them watching the pane to see who got minutes. Fifty on the float was a fight if you let it be a fight. Hask did not let it be a fight. He opened the drawer. He took `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. Second time this week. Same motion as Tuesday, which was the problem he did not know he had.

He fed the top-up cassette. The tooth took it like any other slip. The float jumped to four hundred. The red bar died. The ledger went back to green, fat, a full tank of hours.

He stamped the box truck: three hours, bay six, hash. The cassette came back warm. The driver left with the tape in his shirt pocket like a cigarette he was saving. The arm would lift him at dawn. The leaf would hold. The stamp was honest.

He stamped the flatbed forty because the mill clock was real and the float could take it now. He stamped the day cab sixty because a man who had sat illegal in bay eight still needed a hash that would lift the arm. Both cassettes came back warm. Neither driver thanked him. That was correct. They had paid in wait. The lot had paid in hours. Hask thought the lot had paid.

The CRT did not go back to the ledger.

It sat on a line he had not keyed.

`CLERK HOURS APPLIED`

`REMAINING: 0`

`HASK — REST COMPLETE`

`BAY: BOOTH`

He opened the service menu. He could always get there. `STAMP TEST` fired a blank hash onto a wiped slip. The tube still stamped. The float still read four hundred. `CLERK ID` now carried a rest ticket, same format as a truck: minutes, hash, bay. The bay was the booth. The minutes were what had been left of his shift. The top-up had not been company hours. It had been his remaining hours, spent into the lot so bay six could sleep.

He had not agreed to that. He had agreed to a wage and a stool.

He tried `VOID`.

The menu wanted a confirm. He confirmed. `VOID` opened a second pane that wanted two things at once: a clerk ID with hours on it, and a physical override the booth kept on a hook under the sill. The override was a tagged key, worn, labeled `VOID` in the same grease pencil as the top-up cassette. He had used it twice in eleven months for drivers who had been hashed into the wrong bay. Both times the menu had accepted the key and his ID together. Both times his ID had hours on it.

He put the key in the slot under the tube. The tube took it. The field asked for `CLERK ID`. He entered `HASK`. The field already knew it. The remaining field already said `0`. The phosphor made a bar.

`VOID REQUIRES HOURS ON ID`

He pulled the key and seated it again. Same bar. He tried the employee number from the hire strip inside the drawer. Same bar. He tried `VOID` off the rest line instead of off `CLERK ID`. The second pane added a grey line: `APPLY DAWN`. Grey meant the overlay would do it when the next clerk’s key hit the counter. His key was already in the tube. His key did not count. Dawn keys counted.

A phone on the sill still dialed the district desk. He dialed it. Ring. Ring. A click into a message that had been recorded in a year the overlay still printed in the corner of the tube, the date the county used when it wanted a form to look official. No one picked up. District desks did not pick up for a booth that had already applied the hours.

He hung up. He left the override key in the slot because pulling it felt like giving up on a door that was not a door. The cursor sat there like it was waiting for a different clerk to sit down and type. There was no different clerk. The keys for dawn were not on the counter yet.

He ran `STAMP TEST` again to see if a clean hash would clear the ticket. It printed clean on a wiped slip. The rest line did not move. `HASK — REST COMPLETE` stayed. `BAY: BOOTH` stayed. The float read four hundred. The lot was fine. He was not.

The top-up cassette had not come back. Company tape, booth tape, clerk tape — the deck did not care what the grease pencil said. It had taken the hours off `CLERK ID` and written them onto the lot.

The pane still opened. A second tanker tapped the glass with a ring. Hask took the cassette, fed it, and the tube refused: `CLERK FULL`. He reseated the trimmed leader on a scrap of tape. The tooth was fine. The stamp was fine. It would not spend a spent clerk.

He put the tanker on the sill with a paper square, old method, minutes in pencil. The driver looked at it like a joke that had arrived late.

“Arm takes the hash,” Hask said.

“Then hash it.”

“Tube won’t. I’m full.”

The driver looked at the booth, at the stool, at the paper. “You’re the clerk.”

“I’m a rest ticket,” Hask said. “Bay is the booth. I can’t void it.”

The driver rolled to the arm and put the paper on the dash where a cassette would sit. The arm did not lift. Pencil was not a hash. He idled on the apron until the minutes ran out of him instead of the tape, then left the way he had come, arm still down, two-lane treating him as what he was.

Inside the pane, under the sill card about top-up, a second card in the same company type: `ABANDONED REST FINES THE PLATE`. The plate on the rest line was `CLERK ID`. If he left the booth, the two-lane would bill the ID he still wore. That was why the stool was the bay. He sat.

He could have pulled the tube’s power and let the lot go dark. Dawn would have found a dead stamp and a clerk who had made a point. Bay six would have lost a hash. The flatbed would have lost a hash. The day cab would have lost a hash. The float would have sat in a dark tube at four hundred hours nobody could spend. He did not pull the power. The stamp had been honest when he fed the top-up. It was still honest. It had charged the hours to the ID that fed the tape.

He ran `STAMP TEST` until the hash printed clean. He left the float at four hundred. He left himself on the rest line: `HASK — REST COMPLETE`, `BAY: BOOTH`, `REMAINING: 0`.

The pane stayed shut after the tanker. He did not owe the apron another pencil. The sodium held. The box truck slept on a live hash. The lot had hours. The clerk did not.

He sat the split stool. Occupancy did not care which half. The rest authorization was for a bay that was the booth, already complete. He was inventory the lot had already used. The stamp would not take him as a clerk until the ticket died, and the ticket was a rest, and rests died at their minutes, and his minutes ran until dawn.

He watched bay six. The produce unit cycled. Legal. The leaf would make the dock. The hours in that hash had been sitting on a stool. The ledger did not put that in the phosphor. It put `FLOAT` 400 and `BAY: BOOTH` and a clerk who could not stamp.

He needed to piss and did not leave the bay. Leaving the bay was abandoning a rest. The card had said what that cost. He used the bottle under the sill the night stool kept for weather and put it back. Occupancy was occupancy. The ticket did not require sleep. It required the body in the bay. The bay was the booth. He occupied it.

The lamp stayed on the low click. The tube did not smear. He ran `STAMP TEST` once more, not because he thought it would void him, because he wanted the hash to keep printing true. It did. Honest stamp. Spent clerk. Both true.

He did not lie down. He did not wait in the lot like a driver. Drivers had hashes. He had a rest line with his name on it. The overlay’s `APPLY DAWN` stayed grey. Grey until keys.

Light came up the two-lane far enough to show gravel. The dawn clerk’s hatchback turned in and parked in the gravel that was not a bay. Hask had the pane shut and the menu back on the ledger. The clerk ticket was still his. He heard the hatchback door. He did not get up for the door.

The other set of keys hit the counter.

The dawn clerk read `REST COMPLETE` off the tube and did not ask him to stand. The bay was occupied. She left the keys where a clerk with remaining hours could pick them up.

He was still the rest. The float was still four hundred. Hours somebody had owned. He had owned them. He sat. The ticket held. The keys sat on the counter.
