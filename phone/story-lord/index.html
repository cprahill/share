01 — The Extra Crossing

Rell Pago had the crate on the cart and the last municipal run already called, and Captain Doss would not leave the slip while the weather board stayed red.

The tag was grease-pencil, same hand as always. LARCH POINT. LAST BUS 19:10. Under that a clinic stamp, circle cracked at the edge, footer printed 2442 in a type so small it was almost manners. The pack on the crate’s seam was sweating through the cardboard. Twenty minutes of cold left if you were kind about it.

Rell bumped the cart against the bumper tire and looked at Doss. Doss looked at the board. Red lamp, municipal column, hairline crack in the tin hood with a strip of tape cooked brown. Lake behind him was glass.

“It’s flat,” Rell said.

“Board’s not,” Doss said.

He had his mug in the rinse bucket. That was how he closed a day. The ferry sat in the slip with its engine ticking down, transom paint worn to primer where the name used to be. A boy on the riprap skipped a washer. The washer went a long way and the lake took it without a wrinkle.

Rell checked the strap. One strap, tight enough. The morning truck had dumped the crate late, after the posted run had already taken water, and Joss in the booth had shrugged the way booth people shrugged when the book was already closed. Rell had signed the crate onto the municipal sheet anyway. She had a habit of signing things that still needed to move.

“There’s a person on the far annex,” she said. “Tag says they’ll wait for the 19:10.”

“Tag can wait. I’m not running red.”

“You can see the water.”

“I can see my ticket if I shove off on a red lamp and the desk asks later.” He dried the mug on his shirttail and set it on the bench, handle turned out, done. “Put it in hold. Hold goes Monday.”

Monday was a locker that had already been warm once. Rell did not say that. She put her boot on the lower slat of the cart so it would not ease down the slope.

The hold cage sat under the booth, chain-link and a padlock Joss opened for anyone with a sheet stamp. Inside: two coils of fence wire from a run that had missed its window, a dented cooler with no name, a stack of life jackets that had not been wet this year. If the crate went in there, the pack would finish itself by dark and Monday would be a stamp and a bin. Rell had put a box in that cage last winter and watched the ice turn to a bag. She had told herself she would not do it twice.

She looked at the pump-house at the end of the slip. The key lived on a hook Doss pretended he did not leave out. She had used that key in April, when the sensor iced and the lake was a plate and a load of fencing had to make Larch before the hardware counter locked. Doss had taken that run. He had written her in the book as an assist, not as an override. There was a difference if you wanted a card to stay clean.

She went to the winch first, because a cart with a sweating pack and a slope wanted a line on it whether the boat left or not. The handle had the slick of a thousand palms. She ran the strap through the bow cleat and took up slack until the crate stopped thinking about the water.

“Don’t dress it for a trip we aren’t taking,” Doss said.

“If it slides I get to swim it.”

He almost smiled. He didn’t. The red lamp put a little color on the wet boards. Out in the cut the water was so still the far pines sat in it twice.

Joss leaned out of the booth with a pencil in her teeth. “Desk called. They know you’ve got a late crate. They said hold.”

“I heard hold,” Doss said.

“They said the 19:10 still leaves Larch even if the annex is empty.” Joss took the pencil out. “I told them we knew what a bus was.”

Rell thumbed the clinic stamp. Same circle she had seen on other late boxes. People who used it did not like Monday. The parts counter up the hill had a work order with her name on the Friday line — extra-run cash against a rebuilt starter she had already promised. That was her problem. The pack shining through cardboard was a different one.

“Give me ten minutes,” she said.

“For what.”

“For the board.”

“Board’s the desk’s.”

“Board’s in our pump-house.”

Doss looked at the key. He looked at the lake. He looked at Rell’s card clipped to her jacket, municipal deck, the strip at the bottom still blank where an extra-run confirm would print if a far-side hand signed it.

“You green that lamp and I have to treat it as weather,” he said. “That’s the rule they wrote so they wouldn’t have to come down here.”

“Lake’s flat.”

“I didn’t say it wasn’t.” He picked up the mug and put it down again. “You override, it logs. Extra-run money only pays if the receiver confirms at Larch. You know that.”

“I know that.”

“Then you’re working for a bus.”

“I’m working for a pack that’s turning into a bag of water.”

He breathed in through his nose and jerked his chin at the pump-house, which was as close to permission as he was going to get.

Rell took the key.

The pump-house smelled like wet copper and the soap Doss used on the mug. The weather board hummed the way a fridge hummed when you stood next to it and tried to decide if the sound was new. Municipal column red. Private column dark. No storm code on the strip. No wind number. Just red, and under the red a small hold mark she had to lean in to see: SLIP RESERVE, paid through 19:00.

That was not weather.

She could hang the key back up. She could roll the crate into the cage with the fence wire and the nameless cooler and walk up the hill and tell the parts counter that Friday had become a different day.

She put the key in the override and turned it.

The municipal lamp went green with a cheap little click. The hold mark stayed on the strip, smaller, like a stain that did not care what color you called the room. Rell’s card buzzed against her jacket once and printed the override under her name. The desk would see it when they wanted to.

She hung the key where Doss liked it.

Outside, the green put a different color on the boards. Doss stared at the lamp the way he stared at a gauge that had just lied in a useful direction.

“Weather’s clear,” Rell said.

“You went in there.”

“Lake’s still flat.”

He looked past her, down the cut. Something was moving in the pines’ reflection. A small launch, private paint, coming off idle.

“That’s not Larch traffic,” Doss said.

“Start the extra run. Green is green. You said.”

He hated that she was right in the exact way the desk had built the rule. For a second he kept both hands on the rail and did not step down. Then he stepped onto the ferry and woke the engine. The tick became a working sound. Rell yanked the winch strap off the cleat and rolled the cart to the gate. The pack had gone from sweating to shining. She could feel the cold through the cardboard if she set her palm there and didn’t lie to herself about how much time that meant.

“Far-side confirm,” Doss said, already in the wheel well. “No confirm, no bonus. I don’t argue with the sheet.”

“You’ll get a confirm.”

She said it like a person who had not just seen SLIP RESERVE.

Joss came down from the booth with the extra-run chit and a look that meant she had watched the lamp change color and had decided not to be in the sentence. She clipped the chit to Rell’s card anyway. Procedure was how you stayed friends with a booth.

The private launch came into the cut wider than it needed to, as if the water were a road it had paid for. A woman stood in the bow with a receipt folded hard in her fist. Work jacket. Clinic patch on the shoulder, same cracked circle as the stamp on the crate.

Rell kept rolling.

“That’s our water,” Doss called, not leaving the wheel.

The launch’s skipper — a kid with sunburn in the part of his hair — throttled down and let the hull kiss the outer tire. He had the face of somebody following a paid instruction. The woman stepped onto the slip like she had measured the distance in the car.

“Orland,” she said. “Bex. That’s mine.”

Rell’s hands were on the cart handle. “It’s tagged Larch Point.”

“I tagged it Larch Point.” Bex held up the receipt. Weather desk print, the same tired type as the bus line on the crate. HOLD MUNICIPAL / CINDER SLIP / PAID. Under that a charter number and 2442 in the footer, same shy type as the clinic stamp. “I rode around. I’m not on the far annex. If you take that boat out, you’re taking my pack to an empty bench.”

The pack shone. Rell could smell the cardboard going sweet.

Doss pulled the extra-run throttle back to idle. He did not look surprised enough. That was worse than if he had sworn.

“Board was red,” he said, to Bex, or to the receipt, or to the rule.

“I paid it red. Desk sells that. I didn’t buy a storm. I bought you staying put until I could get here.”

Rell heard the April fencing run in her head and put it down. “You called the booth at noon. You said you’d wait at Larch for the 19:10.”

“I said that when I still thought the morning truck would miss this slip and the crate would already be on the water.” Bex’s voice had the shake of somebody who had been driving with a cooler in the back that was not cold enough. “Truck was late. Your posted run left without it. I paid a hold and I paid a launch and I came around the lake because the clinic on this side will take the pack if I walk it up the hill in the next half hour. Larch will not take a warm bag at 19:10. Larch will throw it in a bin and stamp Monday.”

Joss had come close enough to read the receipt without admitting she wanted to. “Desk didn’t tell us it was a hold-for-pickup.”

“Desk sold me the red lamp. They don’t sell explanations.”

Rell looked at the extra-run chit on her card. Far-side confirm. The line was blank. Bex was not going to Larch. Bex was not going to sign a dock she was not standing on.

“If I take it across anyway,” Rell said.

Bex put her hand on the crate lid, not pulling yet. “Then I lose the pack, and you get a boat ride, and somebody at Larch watches a bus leave with an empty bench. Please don’t help me like that.”

The launch kid called a time. Bex didn’t turn. Doss rubbed his mouth.

“Green’s still green,” he said, and it came out tired. “I can run it. I won’t get paid for a confirm I don’t have. That’s her problem.” He meant Rell. He meant the card.

Rell took her boot off the slat.

The cart rolled one inch toward the ferry gate on its own, the slope still interested. She pulled it back and swung it toward Bex’s feet. The winch line slithered off the corner and hit the boards with a dead sound.

“Take it.”

Bex took it. She had a strap of her own, cleaner than the municipal one, and a little road cooler in the launch that started complaining as soon as they lifted the crate over the gunwale. The pack left a wet print on the cardboard like a hand.

“I can mark received here,” Bex said. She was already looking at Rell’s card, at the blank confirm line. “This side. If that does you any good.”

Doss shook his head before Rell could answer. “Sheet only pays Larch. Desk wrote it that way so we wouldn’t invent docks.”

Bex folded the receipt smaller. “I tried.”

She got back in the launch without waiting to be thanked. The sunburned kid pushed off. The hull drew a line in the glass and then the line filled in.

Doss cut the engine. The ferry settled against the tires the way it had before Rell had gone for the key. Green lamp still on. Municipal column claiming clear weather for a boat that was not going.

“You going to red it,” Rell said.

“I’m going to leave it. Desk can come down and look at their own color. I’m not touching a lamp you already lied to.”

He said it without heat. That was the write-up voice. He took the extra-run chit off her card, looked at the blank confirm, and tucked the chit into his shirt pocket instead of throwing it in the lake. He would need it for the book.

Joss climbed back to the booth and started the closing count she had already started once. The pencil went back in her teeth. The boy on the riprap had run out of washers. He sat on his heels and watched the launch get small along the near shore, not toward Larch, toward the hill clinic and a door that would still be open if Bex walked fast.

Rell coiled the winch line. The coil came out sloppy. She did it again until it was a coil Doss would not comment on. Her card sat heavy on the jacket clip: override, time, no confirm. The strip at the bottom had printed a small spoiled mark, a square punched through the municipal deck line, the kind of mark that followed you to the next extra run and made the desk ask questions they already knew the answers to.

“Friday cash,” Doss said, not looking at her. “You told the parts counter you had an extra-run coming.”

“I’ll tell them Monday.”

“Monday the hold cage will be empty and you’ll still have the mark.” He rinsed the mug a second time, which was how he ended an argument he intended to win later. “You can fix a board. You can’t make the desk pay a dock that isn’t on the sheet.”

Rell looked across the water. Larch Point was a pale cut in the trees, the annex roof a dull tin square. Somewhere over there a bench was already in the shade. The 19:10 would stop because buses stopped even when the person who had meant to sit down had spent the afternoon in a car going the long way around a lake.

“You want me off the slip,” she said.

“I want you off the override list before they call it a habit.” Doss set the mug on the bench, handle out. “Go. I’ve got a green lamp and no run.”

She took the cart, empty now, and pushed it up the slope to the rack. The wheels ticked on the boards. The bumper tire had a wet bite mark from the crate’s corner. She left it.

On the two-lane above the slip the air was warmer than the water had been. The parts counter sat in a cinder block with a window that had been taped twice. The woman inside saw Rell coming without a crate and without the look of a person who had just been paid, and she turned the work order facedown so the Friday line would not have to be a conversation yet.

Rell did not go in.

She walked the shoulder as far as the turnout where you could still see the slip. The ferry was a dark lid on glass. The weather board threw green onto the wet wood like a leftover. Far along the near shore the launch was gone behind a stand of pine. On the other shore Larch kept its tin roof and its timetable. The year on that timetable was the same year as the clinic stamp. Nobody over there was going to read it out loud either.

Rell unclipped her card and looked at the spoiled square until it was only a square. Then she clipped it back and kept walking, up the two-lane, away from a boat that had been ready and a bench that would stay empty and a lamp that would sit green until somebody from the desk came down and called it weather.

02 — Night Heat

Sela Quade had the gate chain in her hand and the first kids already on the stairs when Wick came up from three with the dump slate and a look that meant the kitchen floor was about to trip.

The hour-sign hung on the chain-link, laminated twice, corners soft: ROOF POOL / KIDS 18:00–19:00 / STACK RESIDENTS. Year in the footer, 2442, same shy type as the boiler plate on the landing. Nobody read the year. They read the hour.

Sela hooked the chain open. Jem came through first with a kickboard that had a bite out of the foam. Behind him towels and wet hair and the kind of shouting a roof learned to live with. Oda sat on the bench with her shoes off and her work badge still clipped, which was how you could tell she had come straight up from three.

“Still ours?” Oda said.

“Until seven,” Sela said.

Wick stopped at the landing and did not come onto the deck yet. He had the slate under his arm the way he carried a wrench, not a weapon. Sweat at his collar. Kitchen heat rode the stairwell behind him, a greasy warmth that did not belong next to chlorine.

“Need the pool at twenty past,” he said.

Sela looked at the water. She had skimmed it at twenty to six. The surface had that late-day skin. Fine for an hour. Fine if nobody dumped into it.

“Kids just got here.”

“Kitchen grate’s ticking. I can give you twenty minutes. After that it trips three and I have to put the heat somewhere wet.” He tipped the slate so she could see the line without taking it. DUMP 18:20. POOL. His initials already in the box because the kitchen did not take a maybe. “Pool’s the legal sink. You know that.”

She knew that. In July she had opened the pool valve when the roof was empty enough to argue with and the water had come up warm as dishwater and stayed that way until dark. She had posted CLOSED and sat with the skimmer in her lap while towels went back down the stairs. The desk had sent a note that the roof was a courtesy. She had fought them for the hour in the spring, standing in a lobby that smelled like bleach and wet mail. They had given it to her on the condition that the roof stayed a roof.

Jem was already on the top step of the ladder, waiting for her nod. Sela nodded. He dropped in. The sound was the right sound. Other kids followed because that was how a pool worked if you did not stop it.

Wick stayed on the landing. “Twenty past.”

“I heard you.”

He went back down. The stairwell kept his heat a few seconds after he left.

Sela walked the deck the way she always did in the first five minutes: skimmer once around the shallow end, check the gate latch, check the posted hour so nobody could say they hadn’t seen it. The boiler plate on the landing was older than the sign. Somebody had painted over a crack in it and the paint had yellowed. She had eaten lunch on that landing for two summers.

Oda watched Jem do a bad handstand and come up grinning. “He talks about this all day. They cut the yard hour in the lower court. This is what’s left.”

“I know what’s left.”

She said it shorter than she meant to. Oda didn’t take it badly. People in the stack learned the difference between short and closed.

The kitchen grate on three was not visible from the roof. You felt it. A tick in the rail if you put your palm on the landing post. Sela put her palm there at ten past. The tick was real. Wick had not invented it to ruin an hour.

Morning taps drank from the courtyard fountain four floors down. Everybody in the stack knew that the way they knew which elevator lied.

Sela had never opened the court handle. The pool could be closed and nobody woke up thirsty. A warm reserve put bottles in the lobby and a name on a board.

Jem waved from the deep end. Sela lifted two fingers. He went back to being a kid.

At twelve past the tick in the rail got honest. A faint hair of vapor showed at the mid-stack landing if she leaned. She did not lean long.

Wick came back up at eighteen past, earlier than he had promised, because kitchen floors did not keep promises.

“It’s going,” he said. “I need a sink.”

“Give me the pool until seven.”

“I can’t give you the kitchen until seven. Three trips, I lose the line, I lose the cookers, I lose the dinner the building already paid for.” He held the slate out. One bar left. No speech attached. “Pool or court. That’s the menu.”

Sela looked through the fence. Jem had the kickboard under his chest and was kicking like the water owed him money. A girl with a green towel was teaching a smaller one to blow bubbles. The smaller one spat and laughed and spat again. Oda had her badge unclipped now, spinning it on one finger, off the clock in the only way the stack allowed.

“If I take the pool they lose the hour,” Sela said.

“If you take the court, morning loses the tap.” Wick rubbed his eye with the heel of his hand. “I’m not here to pick your people. I’m here because the grate is going to trip and the dump has to land in water. You pick the water.”

Sela went to the closet.

It was a metal box on the far side of the fence, padlock on her belt so the kids would not treat the handles like toys. Inside, two handles painted so many times the paint had a geology. Left: a strip of blue tape gone black at the edges. Pool. Right: an F scratched into the housing by somebody who had needed to remember in the dark. Fountain. Courtyard. Morning. A third pipe with no handle, capped, leftover from a year when somebody had thought the roof planters could take overflow. The planters had died. The cap had stayed.

Wick stood in the door with the slate against his chest. “Your call if you want it to be. My call if you don’t make one.”

The blue-tape handle was the one she had thrown in July with her mouth shut and her name still in the day box. The other handle was stiffer. She put her shoulder into it. The pipe kicked once against the wall and then ran, a low sound you could feel in your teeth if you were the one who had opened it. Warmth left the stairwell. The rail under her palm went quieter.

Down in the courtyard the dark circle of the fountain coughed and then ran. A skin of steam lifted off water that had been still. The pool did not move. The pool did not warm.

Wick wrote FOUNTAIN over POOL in a print that would photocopy badly. He initialed the change. He did not thank her.

“I’ll be on the landing,” he said. “If the grate keeps climbing I have to come back.”

“If you come back you take the fountain again.”

He looked at her. “If I come back the fountain’s already carrying what it can carry.”

She locked the closet until it was only a door.

The pool stayed the temperature of a decent evening. Jem hauled himself onto the edge and shook his head like a dog. Water hit the concrete and started drying in the shape of him.

“Are you coming in?” he said.

“I don’t come in.”

“That’s a rule you made up.”

“That’s a rule that keeps me employed.”

He thought about that and decided it was adult nonsense and went back under. Oda caught Sela’s eye and mouthed thank you, which was more than the hour usually paid.

Sela sat on the bench with the skimmer across her knees and let the hour be an hour. She did not watch the courtyard. She could hear the fountain change its voice, a fuller sound, like a pot left on. Nobody on the roof remarked on it. Roof people did not look down unless something broke.

The girl with the green towel got the smaller one to float for a count of four. The smaller one swallowed water and came up furious and then wanted to try again. A ball that had been fading since spring bumped the tile and stayed in. Jem attempted a dive that was mostly a fall and surfaced with the kickboard held over his head like a prize he had invented. Sela blew the whistle once, soft, when two kids started a dunking that had an edge. They separated. The water closed over the argument and went back to being water.

At ten to seven she blew the whistle for real. Kids groaned the way kids groaned when the water was still good. She gave them the extra two minutes she always gave them and then she stood at the ladder and counted heads out. Jem tried to negotiate one more jump. Sela pointed at the hour-sign. He climbed out dripping and righteous.

Oda herded towels toward the stairs. “Same tomorrow?”

“Hour’s posted,” Sela said.

She did not say whose name would be next to the hour.

Wick came up as the last kid hit the landing. He had a marker and a strip of tape and the look of a man who had already had this conversation with himself on the stairs.

“Management wants a day attendant who will use the pool sink,” he said. He said it to the fence first, then to her. “They’re posting you night. Gate, skim, no hour. The dump will happen on day shift when the roof is empty. That’s the deal they can live with.”

Sela coiled the chain. The links were warm from the sun that had already left the water. “The court took it. Kitchen’s fine.”

“Kitchen’s fine tonight. Morning the tap’s going to taste like the line. They’ll put bottles in the lobby and they’ll want a person to blame who isn’t the grate.” He stuck the tape to the hour-sign, not covering the hours, covering the small line at the bottom where the attendant initial lived. He wrote NIGHT in block letters because block letters photographed. “I’m sorry it was you. You were the one standing next to the handles.”

“I know how the handles work.”

“I know you do. That’s why they’re moving you.” He capped the marker. “Night pay’s the same. You just don’t get the hour.”

Sela looked at Jem’s wet footprints heading down the stairs, already fading. “The hour wasn’t mine.”

“No. It was theirs.” He tapped the NIGHT tape once so it would stay. “They wanted a dump that didn’t show up in the lobby.”

He went down. She took the NIGHT tape off, looked at the adhesive ghost it left, and stuck it back because peeling it would not unwrite the board in the lobby.

She skimmed once more around a pool nobody was using. The late-day skin came back. She locked the gate. The hour-sign still said 18:00–19:00. The footer still said 2442. The new word at the bottom said who was allowed to stand here when it counted.

Night work was the same roof without voices. Sela came back at eleven and ran the skimmer under the lights and listened to the building tick in a different key. The court fountain kept a little heat after midnight. She could see the steam if she leaned over the north fence. She leaned once and then she didn’t.

A moth beat itself against the deck light. The chain ticked when the wind found it. Sela fished a plaster wrapper off the shallow end and put it in her pocket because the morning attendant — whoever that was going to be — would write a note if the deck was dirty. She sat on the bench until her coffee went cold and the pool held the lights without comment.

At six she went down the stairs with the night keys and a taste in her mouth she was going to blame on the coffee.

The lobby had a folding table in front of the mail slots. Cases of bottles, still in the plastic, a handwritten sign taped to the front: TAP OFF / TAKE TWO / RETURN EMPTIES. Somebody had added a smiley face and then scratched it out. The woman from management who did not live in the stack stood behind the table with a tick sheet. People took bottles with the face they used for fire drills and parking tickets.

Sela took two because the sign said two. The plastic was cold. That was the joke.

Through the courtyard glass the fountain was a ring of dull water. It dribbled. A warm fog sat on the basin the way steam sat on a sink after dishes. Nobody was filling anything from it. A kid she did not know pressed a palm to the glass and pulled it away.

Oda came out of the elevator with Jem and a backpack. Jem saw Sela and lifted the kickboard like a question. He said, “Fountain’s weird.” Oda saw the table and the bottles and the fog on the glass and did not ask the question out loud.

“They posted night on your line,” Oda said.

“I saw.”

“The hour’s still on the sign.”

“The hour’s still on the sign.”

Jem pulled at Oda’s sleeve. Oda put a bottle in his hand and one in her own and steered him toward the street door. She looked back once. Sela lifted two fingers, the same two she had lifted in the deep end, and Oda nodded as if that were a shift they could split.

Wick came through the service door with a wrench and the slate blank. He saw Sela with the bottles and winced like a man who had hoped to miss this particular morning.

“Tap’ll clear by Wednesday if nobody dumps court again,” he said. “Day hire starts at four. She’ll open the pool valve when I ask. Roof stays closed until the water cools, then the hour happens in what’s left.”

“What’s left is late.”

“What’s left is what they paid for.” He set the wrench on the table, not in the way of the bottles. “You working tonight?”

“I’m posted night.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Sela looked at the hour-sign in her head, still taped to a fence she would not open at six. She looked at the warm ring in the courtyard and the plastic in her hands and the kickboard going out the street door.

“I’ll work tonight,” she said.

The management woman ticked a box. Two bottles. Night name. The year on the tap-off sign was the same year as the hour-sign footer, printed small, already ignored.

Sela went out the service door because the street door had kids in it. Behind her the fountain kept dripping like a line that had done its job and did not know how to stop. The roof was six flights up and empty and still posted for an hour that would belong, tomorrow, to whoever was willing to let the pool take the heat.

She climbed anyway. The chain was where she had left it. The water held last night’s sky a little longer than the courtyard had. Sela sat on the bench with two unopened bottles and the skimmer across her knees and waited for eleven, when the voices would be gone and the work would still be hers.
