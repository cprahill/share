Slot Three

story-lord · Marty Brown · revise 1789852165.1823394

The packet said `SLOT 3 / DO NOT PLAY`. Len did not play it. She was taking it to RIDGE HUB.

The case sat in the passenger footwell, plastic that had once held a mix, grease pencil on the spine. The CRT where a radio would have been drew a two-lane and a blinking square twenty-eight miles out. RIDGE HUB, it said, then the square, then a number that ticked down like a parking meter. The square was the job board. The drop paid if the board took the packet and filled a gap.

She kept the van in third because fourth smeared the picture. The dash was analog: oil, amps, a cassette deck with a red tooth that ate the edge of every tape she fed it. The tooth logged the route. If it took a tape, a hub could take her.

That was the job. She had already done one today.

Slot 7 had been a fuel island east of here, a kiosk bolted to a cabinet that used to sell ice. The packet then said `SLOT 7 / KEEP SEAL`. She fed it. The kiosk CRT showed the pump canopy from above: painted lines, a diesel island, a dog on a chain that did not move. Her job was to keep a van-shape between the lines until the meter died. She kept third on the real dash so the picture stayed sharp. The yoke on the kiosk pulled toward the diesel every lap. She let it. Fighting it smeared the cam the same way fourth smeared her own tube.

The kiosk meter died slower than her dash meters died. She held the van-shape off the diesel for three more laps while the number crawled. A pickup rolled through the real island and the cam stuttered; she kept the yoke still until the picture caught up. At zero the kiosk thunked. Slot 7 filled with a node ID that was not hers. The cassette spat back, edge chewed, hubs still warm. The attendant counted forty onto the counter without looking at her face and punched a hash onto a slip. The CRT in her dash kept the hub square. She was still the Len who owned the route.

She asked if Slot 7 paid extra for a clean leader. It did not. Forty was forty. She put the chewed tape in the door pocket with the other chewed tapes and took the cash. The kiosk was already a dark tube. The next courier could have it.

She slept two hours in a rest lot with the oil light on. The CRT she killed to save amps. The tooth she left empty so it would not chew a tape she might still need. She woke with this packet already in the footwell and RIDGE HUB already blinking, twenty-eight miles, meter live. She did not remember writing `SLOT 3 / DO NOT PLAY`. Couriers woke up with packets. The job was to deliver them. Slot 7 had paid and spat. Slot 3 would pay the same way if she fed the tape and filled the gap.

The square ticked. Twenty-eight became twenty-two. She stayed in third. The tooth sat idle with nothing in it, a red notch waiting for the next edge. Oil light off. Amps steady. The two-lane on the CRT matched the two-lane in the windshield, except the windshield did not have a meter.

She ate the last of a gas-station burrito that had gone cold in the cup holder. She did not play the packet. The label was the contract. Play was for people who did not have a drop.

The mall still had a name on the big sign. Letters missing. It read like a cough. She parked in a stall the lot still painted for couriers, faded box, number worn off. Inside, a floor machine hummed. Someone was still getting paid to keep the pattern.

The arcade was the last lit box. RIDGE RUN in yellow letters. A steering yoke instead of a stick. The screen was a CRT. High scores down the right: ten names, ten numbers, node IDs, not initials. Slot three was a gap. The number next to the gap was waiting.

A kid in a work shirt sat on a folding chair with a coil of keys. He did not look at her face. He looked at the cassette case.

“You play or you drop,” he said.

“Drop is play,” Len said.

He shrugged. The keys chimed. “Machine knows. I just lock up.”

She looked at the gap. Slot three had a blank where a node ID should sit, a hard rectangle in the phosphor, waiting. The other slots had names. She did not read them. The gap was the drop. Fill it, keep the route, get the square back on the dash for the next town.

“You going to feed it,” the kid said, not a question.

She set the case on the cabinet lip and popped it. The tape inside had her handwriting on the leader, or handwriting that looked like hers. The red tooth on her dash was a logger. This slot was a mouth. She fed the cassette under the yoke. The tooth in the cabinet took the edge the way hers did, a little too eager, a little too much plastic in the bite.

RIDGE RUN did not show a road. It showed a service lane behind the mall: dumpsters, a chain-link cut, the same two-lane she had just driven, only from above, like a security cam that had learned to be a game. A van-shape sat at the mouth of the lane, her dings copied onto its quarter panel. Her job was to keep that shape in the lane until the meter hit zero and the gap in the list filled.

She could do that. She had done worse with less picture.

The yoke had weight. Not arcade weight. Drop weight. The first pull was the real pothole out back; she had felt it in the van on the way in. The cam showed it a half-second late. She steered for where it would be, not where it was. The van-shape hopped and stayed in the lane.

A number ticked in the corner. Same parking-meter font as her dash. The kid’s keys made a small sound every time he shifted on the chair. He still had not looked at her face.

Lap one she counted dumpsters. Seven. One lid up. The cam wanted her to wait for the lid or clip it. Clipping it smeared the CRT the way fourth did. She waited. The lid came down on its own hydraulics, slow, and she took the gap. Meter: still fat.

Lap two the yoke pulled left on the same pothole. She let it. Fighting it made the phosphor smear into the chain-link, and once you lost the lane the meter froze until you found it again. She had frozen a meter on a drop two towns back and sat there until the hub timed out and paid nothing. She did not freeze this one. She counted the pothole as a thing the cam would always have, like fourth would always smear, like the tooth would always take an edge. You worked around the pull. You did not argue with it.

The van-shape on the cam had her rust at the wheel well. It had the scrape on the bumper from a loading dock in a town that did not put its name on the CRT. She was driving a picture of her own van around a picture of a lane she had already driven. That was the job. The packet went in. The gap filled. You got to keep the route.

Lap three a cart rolled out from behind the last dumpster. Not in the real lane — she would have heard it — but in the cam. She lifted off the yoke the way she lifted off the real pedal in third, and the van-shape slowed without the smear. The cart drifted. She went around it. Meter: half.

The kid said nothing. He was waiting on the machine, not on her.

Lap four she almost overcorrected. The pothole came early, or the cam lagged more than a half-second, and she put in too much yoke. The picture smeared. The two-lane bloomed into white. She dropped her hands, let the yoke find center, waited for the phosphor to settle. The van-shape had kissed the chain-link. The meter had not frozen. She put the shape back in the lane and did not look at the kid.

“Still in,” he said, to the keys.

She counted dumpsters again to have something that was not the smear. Seven. Lid down. Cart gone. The service lane was a loop. RIDGE RUN did not care if she knew that. It cared if the van-shape stayed between the lines.

The meter got thin. Twelve. Eight. The yoke got heavier, or her arms did. She kept third in her head even though there was no gearbox on the cabinet. Fourth smeared CRTs. This was a CRT. She did not ask it for more picture than it had.

Five. Three. The dumpster lid stayed down. The chain-link cut stayed a cut. At zero the machine thunked, the kind of thunk a locker makes when it takes a bag and does not give it back.

Slot three filled: `LEN-4`.

The kid stood up. “Packet’s in,” he said. “You’re done.”

Len waited for the cassette to spit.

It did not.

Slot 7 had spat. Kiosks spat. You got the chewed tape back as proof you had been the drop, and you put it in the door pocket with the others. She waited a breath. Another. The tooth in the cabinet kept its bite. The hubs did not reverse.

She hit the eject under the yoke. The button had travel. The tooth kept turning. The tape stayed in the mouth, leader gone, hubs still. She hit it again. Same travel. Same nothing. She put a thumbnail in the slot the way you did when a deck ate a mix you still wanted. There was no purchase. The plastic was flush. The tooth had the tape, and the tape was the packet, and the packet was in.

“It’ll spit,” she said.

“Packet’s in,” the kid said. He was already looking at the glass door, keys in his hand. “You’re done.”

“I need the tape.”

“Machine kept it,” he said. “That’s how slot three works. I just lock up.”

The screen did not go back to the service lane. It did not go back to RIDGE RUN. It went to a service menu, white on black, the same ugly font her dash used when she held the mode for three seconds.

`ROUTE CERTIFICATE`

Her van’s plate, letter for letter, including the dented character the county had never restamped. Her meter, the number that had just died. A timestamp from two towns back: 04:12, while she had been asleep in a rest lot with the oil light on, the Slot 7 cash still in her jacket, this packet not yet in the footwell, or already in the footwell and she had not looked.

`REPLACED BY LEN-5`

`RECALL: THIS NODE`

The kid was at the glass door with the keys.

“You knew,” she said.

“I know slot three,” he said. “I don’t know you. There’s a Len-5 in the lot. Came in on four. Same van paint. Different tooth on the deck.”

He locked the door from the inside and then unlocked it, a habit, and went out. The coil of keys stayed loud. He did not wait to see if she followed. Slot three was full. His job was to lock up when it filled.

Len looked at the menu until the phosphor made a bar of the words. `ROUTE CERTIFICATE` did not ask her to confirm. It had already confirmed. The timestamp was a sleep she remembered: oil light, rest lot, burrito foil, the CRT dark because she had killed it to save amps. Someone had written a packet in that dark, or the deck had, or the hub had, and she had driven it here because that was the drop.

She walked to the lot.

There was a van. Same dings. Same rust at the wheel well. Same scrape from the loading dock. Same third-gear rattle when the other driver bumped it into idle. A woman with Len’s jacket cut sat in it eating a gas-station hot dog, CRT glowing the next square. The deck in that dash had a tooth. It was red. It was not Len’s red. Newer plastic, less chew in the notch, a clean bite that had not yet learned every tape.

Len walked as far as the stall paint and stopped. The other van’s oil light was off. Hers, in the next stall, was already ticking the way it ticked when the engine was cold. Same paint fade on the sliding door. Same crease above the wheel. The bumper scrape lined up if you squinted and did not if you did not: a little longer on the other van, or a little newer, metal still bright in the groove.

She almost spoke. The name was in her mouth. The plate was in her mouth. She did not say either. The other woman did not wave. She did not have to. She had a square on her CRT. Len’s CRT, through the glass of her own van, still showed RIDGE HUB with the meter at zero.

The packet had been a recall. Len had delivered it herself because that was the job, and the job did not care which Len still thought she was the original.

The other van’s idle was her idle. The dings lined up. The tooth did not. That was the proof. She could have asked for a face and gotten one. She did not ask. The hot dog steamed. Mustard on the paper. The other woman looked at the next square, not at the stall, and reached for the gear without looking at the lot. Third. Always third. The rattle was the rattle. The tooth in that deck sat still, a packet already in it or a packet not needed yet. Len’s tooth, empty, had started to turn.

Len went back in. The cabinet had already eaten the cassette. RIDGE RUN was a road again, a clean lane, waiting for the next drop. Slot three still said `LEN-4`. The gap was gone. She was on the board. That was the whole prize.

The kid had not gone far. He was counting the coil, locking a grate that did not need locking. She asked if she could buy a blank tape. He sold her one from a drawer of blanks that had never been blanks, just wiped. The leader was too white. The hubs had no dust. She paid cash from the Slot 7 forty. She did not ask for a receipt. He did not offer one. He did not look at her face. He looked at the empty slot under the yoke, which was empty now, which was his problem until the next courier.

Out at her van the analog dash was already ticking. Oil. Amps. The deck’s red tooth was turning with nothing in it, chewing air, logging a dead route because that was what the tooth did when the certificate was gone. She sat with the door open and listened to it. It had a rhythm. It had always had a rhythm. Now the rhythm had no tape to spend. She put her palm on the dash over the deck. The plastic was warm. The tooth did not care whose palm it was.

The other van pulled out toward the next square. Same third. Same rattle. She did not follow. She did not go back in to the yoke. She put the wiped cassette in the deck. It took. The tooth found the white leader and started eating what had already been eaten once. No hash printed on her CRT. No plate. No meter. The logger had a tape and no certificate to write onto it.

The CRT drew the two-lane with no square. Just road. No RIDGE HUB. No meter. The job board had let her go by taking her. She sat long enough to see if the square would come back if she waited. It did not. The two-lane on the tube was only the two-lane. The windshield agreed.

She started in third. Fourth would smear a picture she no longer needed, and she kept the habit anyway. The oil light stayed off for a mile, then came back, same as always. She did not smash the deck. She did not call anyone. There was no number on a recall that wanted the person it had just spent.

She drove the unlit stretch with the tooth working. The wiped tape had no route on it. It still had an edge. The tooth still wanted the edge. When the first pass ran out the hubs clicked and the tooth chewed air and then found the leader again, because a wiped tape is all leader if you never recorded a route.

The CRT found a different blink, smaller, unlabeled. It sat off the two-lane like a hub and like a hole. She did not know which. She did not steer for it yet. She watched the tooth and the tube together: if the blink was a hub, a live certificate would have given her a meter. The meter stayed gone. The blink stayed a blink. Behind her, slot three held `LEN-4`. The board still had her name. The certificate did not. The world could mint a Len-5 with a cleaner tooth and a live square and leave this body moving, listed, uncertified.

The next town would treat her as empty. She was still moving. She kept third. The unlabeled blink got a little closer. She did not put it in the meter. She had no meter. She had a board that said she had been the drop, and a dash that said she was still listed, and a tooth that would log whatever she fed it now that the hub no longer owed her a square. The oil light held. She drove.

The Float

story-lord · Marty Brown · revise 1789852195.639087

Hask stamped driver cassettes so the arm would lift. That was the job. The CRT in the booth was the ledger: bay, minutes, plate, a hash. Without a live hash the two-lane counted idle as a violation, and the lot’s arm stayed down on the way out.

The float kept the tube from going red. Float was hours. Hours somebody owned. The company bought them so the bays could fill. When the number got thin you felt it in the pane before you saw it on the phosphor. A clerk with remaining hours could stamp. A clerk without them could not. The tube did not explain that on the first day. It explained it when the number ran out.

He kept the booth lamp on the low click because the high click smeared the tube. The tube sat where a ticket window used to be, four lag screws. It did not draw a road. It drew the ledger. Nine bays on a gravel apron. One sodium live over the apron, enough to see plates.

A log slip was a cassette. Drivers slid it through the pane. Hask fed it to the deck under the tube. The tooth took the edge. The CRT wrote rest onto the tape — bay, minutes, hash — and the cassette came back warm. That was the ticket.

He had the job because he could keep the stamp honest.

First hour, the lower-left lag screw walked the way it walked when the night had been cold. The tube sat a hair off square and the hash printed fuzzy. He snugged it with the wrench he kept under the hot plate. One turn. The ledger sharpened. Service menu, white on black: `STAMP TEST`, `FLOAT`, `CLERK ID`. He had been on `CLERK ID` for eleven months. `FLOAT` read four hundred. That was a full tank of hours the lot was allowed to spend.

The first truck of the shift was a clean stamp. Flatbed, bay four, twenty minutes. The driver had a log with a square leader and a plate that matched the door. Hask fed it. The tooth took a polite bite. The CRT wrote bay four, twenty, hash. The cassette came back warm. He passed it through the pane. The arm lifted when the flatbed rolled. Float ticked 400 to 380. The stamp was honest. The hours were somebody’s. He did not ask whose.

He held the warm cassette a second on his side of the pane, the way you did when you were teaching a new clerk the difference between a live hash and a cold slip. There was no new clerk. There was the heat. Live hash had heat. He passed it. The driver put it in the dash deck without looking, which was correct. The arm believed the hash, not the face.

Ordinary night after that. Pickup in bay one, fifteen minutes. A reefer that only wanted a hash refresh, ten minutes, already legal, just topping the slip so the two-lane would not argue. The float came down in small bites. The pane stayed easy. The arm stayed a machine that believed him. He did not open `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. He did not need it while the number was fat.

The afternoon clerk had left a ghost bar in the burn if you looked from the side. Uncleared hash. Usual. Hask did not wipe it. Wiping it meant a menu he did not need while the float was fat. The ghost would spend itself. That was what uncleared lines did.

A reefer took bay two after the sodium flickered. The driver had a split knuckle and a log that had already been through two other stamps, the tape edge fuzzy. She wanted forty minutes. Hask fed it. The tooth chewed, then took. Forty minutes, bay two, hash. The cassette came back warm enough. He passed it. She put it in the same pocket as her gloves and did not wait for the pane to close. The float ticked from ninety to fifty.

Ninety. He looked at the number twice. The clean stamps had not spent that much. The ghost bar had. The afternoon clerk had left hours on a line that was still burning, and the tube had finished spending them when bay two hashed. Fifty left. Fifty was not a night.

The next driver was already at the pane. Day cab, bay eight, a log that wanted sixty. Hask shook his head before the cassette was all the way through.

“Fifty on the float,” he said. “I can give you fifty. You sit the last ten dark or you wait.”

The driver held the cassette in the slot so the pane could not close. “I got a live hash from the last lot.”

“Last lot isn’t this arm,” Hask said. “This arm wants this hash. I can stamp fifty. I can’t stamp sixty.”

“Then stamp fifty and I’ll sit.”

“You’ll sit with a hash that dies at fifty. Two-lane still counts the rest. Arm won’t lift you out legal.”

The driver looked at the sodium, at the dark bays, at the tube. “Who gets the fifty.”

“Whoever I feed,” Hask said. “You’re here.”

“There’s a box coming. I heard him on the channel. Three hours.”

“Then you want me to hold fifty for a man who isn’t here.”

The driver shoved the cassette the rest of the way. “Stamp it. I’ll take the fine before I take a dark bay.”

Hask did not stamp it. He set the cassette on the counter on the hot-plate side and let the pane sit open. The float sat at fifty, red not yet, but the bar was not the fat green it had been at four hundred. `FLOAT` in the service menu would still say the number. The number was the hours the lot had left to sell. After that the tube went red and the arm stopped believing stamps.

The day cab idled in bay eight without a hash. Illegal. Quieter than a fight. The driver rapped the pane once more. Hask pointed at the number on the tube. Fifty. The driver swore and took the cassette back unstamped and sat in the cab with the engine on, burning his own minutes instead of the lot’s.

Hask could have stamped fifty and let the two-lane fine the rest. That was a clerk who wanted the pane empty. He wanted the stamp honest. Fifty minutes of rest that died at fifty was a lie you handed a man in a warm cassette. He did not hand it.

The drawer held a company cassette, grease pencil: `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. Hask had used it twice this week. Monday after a cattle truck ate three hours the afternoon clerk had not logged. Wednesday when a wreck on the two-lane stacked four sleepers and the float hit red before midnight. Both times he fed the tape, the float jumped, the arm kept lifting, and he got off the stool at dawn with a wage. The top-up put hours back. The hours came from the company. That was how he had understood it.

He looked at the cassette in the drawer and did not take it yet. Fifty was not a night, but it was not zero. Zero was when you had no choice. He still had a choice. The day cab burned fuel in bay eight and did not come back to the pane.

A van held third across the apron and did not take a bay. Dash CRT, empty slot chewing air. The arm was up. Hask wrote nothing.

The box truck took bay six while the day cab was still deciding. The driver had a log that wanted three hours. He slid it through like a man who had already paid. The tube put up a red bar: `FLOAT LOW`.

Fifty would not cover three hours. Fifty would not cover an hour. The box truck’s reefer unit was already cycling. The driver tapped the pane with a knuckle. Behind him the day cab’s engine kept burning minutes that were not on any tape.

“Three hours,” he said. “I got produce. I got a window.”

Hask showed him the bar. The driver had seen red bars. He had not come here to sit in one. Hask could stamp nothing that big. He could stamp nothing honest without hours. The red bar was the tube saying the lot was out of other people’s time.

“Wait,” Hask said.

“Produce doesn’t wait.”

“Float’s low. I stamp you three hours off fifty, the hash dies before your window, and the arm still won’t lift you legal. You sit a lie.”

The driver put his palm on the pane. “Then don’t sit me a lie. Top it up.”

“You top up,” the driver said. “Booths top up. I know this lot.”

The day cab’s driver was out of the cab now, standing in the sodium, watching the pane to see who got minutes. Fifty on the float was a fight if you let it be a fight. Hask did not let it be a fight. He opened the drawer. He took `TOP UP / BOOTH 9`. He had used it twice this week. The third time was the same motion.

He fed the top-up cassette. The tooth took it like any other slip. The float jumped to four hundred. The red bar died. The ledger went back to green, fat, a full tank of hours.

He stamped the box truck: three hours, bay six, hash. The cassette came back warm. The driver left with the tape in his shirt pocket like a cigarette he was saving. The arm would lift him at dawn. The produce would keep. The stamp was honest.

The day cab came back to the pane. Hask stamped him sixty because the float could take it now. 400 minus 180 minus 60. The numbers were the lot’s again. The day cab’s cassette came back warm. The driver did not thank him. He took the hash and the bay and shut the door.

The CRT did not go back to the ledger.

It sat on a line Hask had not keyed.

`CLERK HOURS APPLIED`

`REMAINING: 0`

`HASK — REST COMPLETE`

`BAY: BOOTH`

He opened the service menu. He could always get there. `STAMP TEST` fired a blank hash onto a wiped slip. The tube still stamped. The float still read four hundred. `CLERK ID` now carried a rest ticket, same format as a truck: minutes, hash, bay. The bay was the booth. The minutes were what had been left of his shift. The top-up had not been company hours. It had been his remaining hours, spent into the lot so bay six could sleep.

He had not agreed to that. He had agreed to a wage and a stool.

He tried `VOID`.

The menu wanted a confirm. He confirmed. `VOID` opened a second pane, uglier: `CLERK ID` with remaining hours, a field, a cursor. He entered his ID. The field already knew it. The tube paused. The phosphor made a bar.

`REMAINING: 0`

`VOID` wanted a clerk with remaining hours. He was the clerk. He was spent.

He backed out. He went in again. Confirm. Field. ID. Same bar. He tried the ID with a space, without a space, with the eleven-month code the menu used on `STAMP TEST`. The tube did not care how he typed a spent clerk. It wanted hours on the ID. His ID had a rest ticket. Rest tickets do not void rest tickets.

He tried `VOID` off the rest line instead of off `CLERK ID`. Same second pane. Same field. Same `REMAINING: 0`. The cursor sat there like it was waiting for a different clerk to sit down and type. There was no different clerk. Dawn was hours out. The keys for dawn were not on the counter yet.

He ran `STAMP TEST` again to see if a clean hash would clear the ticket. It printed clean on a wiped slip. The rest line did not move. `HASK — REST COMPLETE` stayed. `BAY: BOOTH` stayed. The float read four hundred. The lot was fine. He was not.

The top-up cassette had not come back. Company tape, booth tape, clerk tape — the deck did not care what the grease pencil said. It had taken the hours off `CLERK ID` and written them onto the lot.

The pane still opened. A tanker tapped the glass with a ring. Hask took the cassette, fed it, and the tube refused: `CLERK FULL`. He tried the lag screw. It was snug. He reseated nothing. The tooth was fine. The stamp was fine. It would not spend a spent clerk.

He put the tanker on the hot-plate side of the counter with a paper square, old method, minutes in pencil. The driver looked at it like a joke that had arrived late.

“Arm takes the hash,” Hask said.

“Then hash it.”

“Tube won’t. I’m full.”

The driver looked at the booth, at the stool, at the paper. “You’re the clerk.”

“I’m a rest ticket,” Hask said. “Bay is the booth. I can’t void it.”

The driver did not take the pencil square as a fight. He took it as a thing that would not lift the arm. He rolled to the arm anyway and put the paper on the dash where a cassette would sit. The arm did not lift. Pencil was not a hash. He idled on the apron until the minutes ran out of him instead of the tape, then left the way he had come, arm still down, two-lane treating him as what he was.

Hask could have pulled the tube’s power and let the lot go dark. Dawn would have found a dead stamp and a clerk who had made a point. The box truck in bay six would have lost a hash. The day cab would have lost a hash. The float would have sat in a dark tube at four hundred hours nobody could spend. He did not pull the power. The stamp had been honest when he fed the top-up. It was still honest. It had charged the hours to the ID that fed the tape.

He ran `STAMP TEST` until the hash printed clean. He left the float at four hundred. He left himself on the rest line: `HASK — REST COMPLETE`, `BAY: BOOTH`, `REMAINING: 0`.

The pane stayed locked after the tanker. He did not owe the apron another pencil. The sodium held. The box truck slept on a live hash. The lot had hours. The clerk did not.

He sat on the split stool. The vinyl had the split it had always had. The rest authorization was for a bay that was the booth, already complete. He was inventory the lot had already used. The stamp would not take him as a clerk until the ticket died, and the ticket was a rest, and rests died at their minutes, and his minutes ran until dawn.

He watched bay six. The box truck slept on the hash Hask had bought with the shift. The produce unit cycled. Legal. The day cab’s hash would die later and the arm would lift him because the float had hours in it now. Those hours had been sitting on a stool. The ledger did not put that in the phosphor. It put `FLOAT` 400 and `BAY: BOOTH` and a clerk who could not stamp.

He did not lie down. Rest tickets did not require sleep. They required you to occupy the bay. The bay was the booth. He occupied it. The lamp stayed on the low click. The tube did not smear. `STAMP TEST` stayed clean if he ran it. He ran it once more, not because he thought it would void him, because he wanted the hash to keep printing true. It did. Honest stamp. Spent clerk. Both true.

The split in the vinyl caught the back of his thigh the way it always had. He did not shift off it. Shifting was what you did when you still had a shift. He had a rest. He sat the rest. The hot plate clicked once and went quiet. He left it. His hours were already in bay six.

Light came up the two-lane. The dawn clerk’s hatchback turned in and parked in the gravel that was not a bay. Hask had the pane locked and the menu back on the ledger. The tube was ready. The clerk ticket was still his. He heard the hatchback door. He did not get up for the door.

The other set of keys hit the counter.

He was still the rest. The float was still four hundred. Hours somebody had owned. He had owned them. He sat. The vinyl held. The ticket held. The keys sat on the counter where a clerk with remaining hours could pick them up.
